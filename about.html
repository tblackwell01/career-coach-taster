The framework

Seven factors. One honest conversation.

The Factors of Career Choice framework began with a simple observation: most career tools ask what you want to do. Very few ask who you are, what shaped you, and whether the choices you are making are actually yours.

The framework identifies seven factors that shape every significant career decision a person makes. Some are about raw material — what you were born oriented toward, what you have built, what draws your attention. Some are about deeper influences — your values, the families that handed you your first map of the world. Some are about direction and timing — where you are going, and what this specific moment of your life requires.

None of the factors is static. All of them change across a life. The framework is not a one-time diagnostic. It is a template to return to — when something shifts, when you feel stuck, when a life transition changes what is possible or necessary. Career planning equals life planning equals career planning.

Factor 1
Abilities
What you are naturally oriented toward before anyone taught you. The capacities that were already there.
Factor 2
Skills
What you have built. The crucial distinction between skills built by desire and skills built by obligation.
Factor 3
Interests
What draws your attention and how that has evolved. Interests reveal what they were always pointing at.
Factor 4
Values
Your internal compass. Both ethical values and life-stage values — and the gap between what you say you value and what your decisions reflect.
Factor 5
Family
The three families — of origin, of choice, and of work. The scripts handed to you before you knew you were learning them.
Factor 6
Goals
Where you are going — and who decided that. The most important question is not what you want but whether you chose it.
Factor 7
Developmental Stage
What this specific moment of your life actually requires. The same person at 24, 38, 52, and 68 needs very different things from their work — and the framework honors that.
The research foundation

Grounded in decades of vocational and clinical science.

The Factors of Career Choice framework draws on a substantial body of research across vocational psychology, career development theory, clinical psychology, and behavioral science. The framework was designed to make that research accessible and actionable in real career conversations.

Super, D.E. (1980). A life-span, life-space approach to career development. Journal of Vocational Behavior.
The foundational life-span career development model that places values at the core of career identity and recognizes that career decisions evolve across developmental stages.
Kristof-Brown, A. et al. (2005). Consequences of individuals' fit at work. Personnel Psychology.
Meta-analysis of 172 studies on person-organization fit — one of the most robust predictors of career satisfaction and organizational commitment in the literature.
Arieli, S., Sagiv, L., & Roccas, S. (2020). Values at work. Applied Psychology: An International Review.
Meta-analysis of 45 studies across 23,000+ workers finding that value-based fit produces a 0.41 correlation with job satisfaction and 0.56 with organizational commitment.
Schwartz, S.H. (1992 & 2012). Theory of basic human values. Advances in Experimental Social Psychology; Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.
The foundational values circumplex model distinguishing stable ethical values from situational life-stage values — the research basis for the Values factor.
Bowen, M. (1978). Family Therapy in Clinical Practice. Jason Aronson.
The foundational family systems theory underlying the Family factor, including family scripts, assigned roles, and intergenerational transmission of beliefs about work.
Minuchin, S. (1974). Families and Family Therapy. Harvard University Press.
Structural family therapy — the clinical framework for understanding how family structure shapes individual behavior and career decisions.
Whiston, S.C. & Keller, B.K. (2004). The influences of the family of origin on career development. The Counseling Psychologist.
Comprehensive review of how family of origin shapes career development — the most directly relevant empirical foundation for the Family factor.
Gallup (2024). State of the Global Workplace Report.
Employees whose personal values align with their organization's purpose are more than four times as likely to be engaged at work.
Holmes, T.H. & Rahe, R.H. (1967). The Social Readjustment Rating Scale. Journal of Psychosomatic Research.
The validated life stressor scale incorporated into the intake questions to give the AI coach meaningful context about the user's current life circumstances.

What this tool is and is not.

The Factors of Career Choice experience is a career and life reflection tool grounded in behavioral science and vocational psychology. It is designed to help people think more clearly and honestly about the factors that shape their career choices.

This tool is not therapy.

The Factors of Career Choice experience is not therapy, counseling, psychological treatment, or a substitute for a licensed mental health professional. Tyler Blackwell is a workforce strategist and career coach. Dr. Richard Blackwell is a psychologist and life coach. Neither the tool nor its creators are providing clinical mental health services through this platform.

The AI coach within this tool is powered by large language model technology. It is not a licensed therapist, psychologist, counselor, or mental health professional of any kind. Its reflections are intended to support self-examination and career clarity — not to diagnose, treat, or provide mental health care.

If you are experiencing significant emotional distress, depression, anxiety, grief, trauma, or any mental health challenge, seeking support from a qualified mental health professional is strongly encouraged. The conversations you have in this tool are not a substitute for that support.
